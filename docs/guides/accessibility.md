# Sensory control in Stims

Stims is built so you decide how much is happening on screen, how it changes, and when it stops — not just which preset is playing.

## What you control

- **Source and start.** Nothing plays until you pick an audio source and start it yourself.
- **Intensity and motion.** Quality and motion settings scale how much is on screen. If your operating system is set to reduce motion, Stims follows that automatically.
- **How presets change.** Cut switches instantly. Blend crossfades over a duration you set, from half a second to eight — you decide how abrupt or gradual a transition is.
- **Stop.** One action stops everything immediately.
- **Where you left off.** Your last session and settings are saved on your device, so returning to a setup you liked doesn't mean rebuilding it.
- **The rules, not just the preset.** The live editor lets you change how the visuals respond to the music while it's running, in real time — not just pick from a list of looks.

## Why the controls are built this way

Three things distinguish Stims from picking a video and hitting play:

- **The visuals respond to the sound, not just alongside it.** Measured properties of the audio — frequency bands, transients, beat timing — drive motion and color as the music plays, not on a preset loop.
- **You can change the response itself, not just what it's responding to.** The live editor edits the actual rules connecting sound to visuals while they're running.
- **Transitions are a separate control from intensity.** Cut and Blend, and the blend duration, let you set how abrupt or gradual a change feels independent of how bright or busy either side of it is.

## Who this is for

Anyone who wants a visualizer, first. For some people — including autistic and ADHD people — having this degree of control over the pace, intensity, and predictability of sensory input is, as part of managing sensory environments generally, linked to more comfort with stimulation, not less.[^1] That's a general finding about control and predictability, not a claim that Stims changes any particular outcome for any particular person — sensory preferences vary a lot from person to person, and what helps one person may not help another.

## What this isn't

Stims isn't a clinical or therapeutic tool. Nothing here is medical advice, and these controls aren't a treatment for anything — they're just controls, available to everyone who uses Stims.

[^1]: See [`docs/LITERATURE.md`](../LITERATURE.md) and [`docs/SENSORY_ACCESSIBILITY.md`](../SENSORY_ACCESSIBILITY.md) for the underlying citations and their limits. Some of this is general research (not Stims- or autism-specific); the autism-specific finding it's closest to (Unwin et al., self-controlled vs. automatically-cycling sensory-room equipment) is a single study, not a settled result; and whether it applies to Stims specifically is an open question we haven't tested.
